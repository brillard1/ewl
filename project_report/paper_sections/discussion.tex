\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why does frontier width determine noise robustness?}
On Aircraft, 100 near-identical variants keep decision boundaries tightly
packed, so a substantial fraction of clean samples remain actively declining
at any step, yielding a strong positive progress signal.
A corrupted label cannot produce sustained decline --- the rank-$r$ LoRA
update cannot reconcile it with consistent gradients from visually similar
correct classes --- so its loss stagnates and $s_i^{\text{rel}} \to 0$.
The frontier is wide enough that velocity cleanly separates corrupted from
informative samples.
On CUB-200 and Stanford Dogs, classes are more separable: clean samples are
learned quickly, the frontier shrinks early, and noisy samples stagnate
alongside hard clean samples near boundaries, so EWL cannot distinguish them
and occasionally upweights mislabelled examples.
Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism} confirms this: on Aircraft the per-sample
(EMA, velocity) clusters separate and the clean/noisy weight-separation score
grows positive, while on CUB and Dogs the clusters overlap and the score stays
near or below zero.

\paragraph{When to use EWL.}
EWL is appropriate when all three hold: (i)~high inter-class similarity
sustaining a wide frontier; (ii)~suspected label noise; (iii)~the $5.9\times$
step-time overhead is acceptable.
Avoid it when classes are visually separable (narrow frontier, signal
non-discriminative), under class imbalance (the velocity mechanism is
frequency-blind), or in single-pass/streaming settings (the EMA never
converges).
Cost-benefit notes, runtime diagnostics, and extended limitations are in
Appendix~\ref{app:discussion}.
